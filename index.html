As the 2024 presidential campaign ramped up, President Donald Trump stood before a crowd in the battleground state of Wisconsin, making a case for why voters ought to send him back to the White House for a second term.

The demand, which would have stripped the department’s governance away from its faculty members and installed an external chair, was one of nine requirements for the University to begin talks to restore federal funding the White House canceled days earlier. But it was immediately recognized as the most invasive. Though universities, including Columbia, had placed departments under receivership in the past, they had done so only of their own volition, as a last resort to address severe dysfunction.

Sprinkled in between attacks on his Democratic opponent, then-President Joe Biden, for his handling of the economy, immigration, and crime, another issue unexpectedly center stage in Trump’s remarks: Columbia’s response to pro-Palestinian campus protests.

With it, a playbook to scrutinize higher education began to materialize.

The Wakeusha, Wisconsin, rally was held on May 1, 2024, hours after the New York Police Department swept occupied Hamilton Hall and arrested over 100 pro-Palestinian protesters the night of April 30, 2024. It was the climax of a wave of pro-Palestinian protests at college campuses across the country that had been sparked by Columbia’s “Gaza Solidarity Encampment” two weeks earlier on April 17, 2024.

Later in the speech, Trump told the crowd, “To every college president I say: Remove the encampments immediately, vanquish the radicals, and take back our campuses for all of the normal students who want a safe place from which to learn,”

For Trump, the events that occurred approximately 900 miles away in Morningside Heights the night before stood out as another episode that underscored the need for an iron fist on all areas of American society—including universities.

Nine months later, back in the Oval Office, Trump would act on that promise.

And Columbia would be caught in the crosshairs.

1 year since landmark $221 million settlement with Trump administration, Columbia heads along a murky road

By Joseph Zuloaga

July 23, 2026

One year ago, Columbia announced it had struck a landmark $221 million settlement with the Trump administration, restoring most of the $400 million in funding that Washington slashed four months prior.

The deal, spanning 22 pages and signed by top Trump cabinet officials and a member of Columbia’s board of trustees, closed pending Title VI and Title VII investigations over antisemitism allegations at Columbia and marked the end to one of the most aggressive periods of federal scrutiny in the University’s recent history.

The White House called it “a historic settlement” aimed to “restore fairness, merit, and safety in higher education.”

But the sweeping terms bound Columbia to policy changes across admissions, hiring processes, protests, disciplinary measures, and antidiscrimination compliance—upending the federal government’s decades-long relationship with one of the oldest universities in the nation.

As news of the deal reverberated across Morningside Heights and beyond, igniting a groundswell of criticism that settlement amounted to capitulation, Columbia pointed to a key clause in the deal: “No provision of this agreement, individually or taken together, shall be construed as giving the United States authority to dictate faculty hiring, university hiring, admissions decisions, or the content of academic speech.”

“This was our north star, and we did not waver from it,” the University wrote in a web page, referring to the line. “Columbia’s governance remains in our control. The federal government will not dictate what we teach, who teaches, or which students we admit.”

Former acting University President Claire Shipman, CC ’86, SIPA ’94, made a similar case in her first interview with Spectator the day of the settlement.

“I think it’s easy to cast things in really binary terms, and capitulation versus fighting,” Shipman said, later explaining her belief that Columbia was “able to craft an agreement that is in line with our values and doesn’t cross any of the red lines we articulated.”

But reception to the deal outside Low Library was colder. Dozens of students, faculty, and alumni criticized the settlement and its potential precedent for federal encroachment on academic freedom.

Under the settlement the federal government was granted access to “all Columbia staff, employees, facilities, documents, and data related to the Agreement,” barring those protected by legal privileges.

In the year since the monumental signing , the barrage of attacks from Washington, D.C., aimed at Morningside Heights appears to have subsided. But, among Columbia affiliates, there remains a fervent sentiment that the University has been permanently rattled by federal scrutiny and is lying at a crossroads, uncertain about what could come next.

“Columbia’s resolution agreement with the federal government explicitly protects the University’s operational independence, academic freedom, and our ability to serve our students. The agreement ensures that we can continue to pursue our core academic mission without compromising our fundamental values,” a University spokesperson wrote in a statement to Spectator.

The spokesperson added, “Advancing excellence in the areas of teaching, research, patient care, and scholarship is core to the University’s mission and proceeding in the normal course.” The University did not respond to Spectator’s request to obtain comment from Jim Glover, a dean at the Climate School who serves as Columbia’s resolution administrator overseeing the implementation of the deal’s provisions, on how he assess Columbia’s compliance with the deal.

The White House, the Departments of Justice, Education, and Health and Human Services, and Bart Schwartz, the former third-party monitor tasked with overseeing Columbia’s compliance with the settlement’s provisions, did not respond to Spectator’s requests for comment on how they each assess Columbia’s compliance with the terms of the deal. Charles Cooper, the current monitor, declined to be interviewed.

“I don’t think Columbia ever stood up for itself,” Christopher Brown, professor of history, told Spectator. “And as a consequence, by not standing up for itself, it didn’t stand up for its people.”

‘A gun to the University’s head’

By the time Columbia signed its settlement, it was not the first Ivy League school to broker an agreement with federal officials to restore critical funding. Twenty-two days earlier, the University of Pennsylvania had done so to restore $175 million in federal funds. Brown University followed Columbia’s suit, settling a week later.

[Read more: ‘It is a shakedown’: Columbia, Penn, Brown settle with government as peer Ivy League institutions remain in crosshairs]

Still, Columbia’s deal—which, unlike Penn’s deal, included a monetary sum—ignited a firestorm of criticism among University affiliates on the deterioration of Columbia’s relationship with the federal government.

“The Trump administration had a gun to the University’s head,” Virginia Page Fortna, professor of U.S. foreign and security policy and the new president of Columbia’s chapter of the American Association of University Professors, said in an interview with Spectator. “And while I think the University did not stand up for itself in ways that it could have, I think it’s also important to keep the criticism on the Trump administration for putting that gun to its head in the first place.”

Fortna also acknowledged that “it was a difficult set of decisions that anybody in leadership at Columbia had to make.”

Tensions between Columbia and D.C. had predated the second Trump administration, beginning when the House Committee on Education and the Workforce announced its investigation into antisemitism at the University in February 2024. From there, the University’s top brass faced escalating pressure—first from Republicans on Capitol Hill, and later from the Trump White House.

[Read more: Columbia hoped to ‘keep heads down.’ A Republican trifecta could intensify federal scrutiny.]

Reinhold Martin, professor of architecture and former president of Columbia-AAUP, believes that University administrators “misjudged their adversary.”

In his first 100 days, Trump and his closest circle of advisors began their swift push to overhaul the federal government by signing a flurry of executive orders. Many of these—including orders aimed at ending diversity, equity, and inclusion initiatives, and calling for “alien students” to be reported if they engaged in antisemetic incidents—served as harbingers for the subsequent pressure that schools like Columbia would face.

[Read more: Columbia alters DEI statements on University web pages amid Trump executive orders]

One of those advisers with one of the largest purviews over the crafting of the second Trump domestic agenda is White House Deputy Chief Stephen Miller, who approved of pulling federal funding from Columbia, according to a January profile from The Atlantic, and keenly observed how successful that tactic would be.

Brown said the federal government successfully identified Columbia’s federal funding as a chokepoint, calling it a “crude but effective way to attack our University.”

Pointing to Miller’s role in Trump’s mass deportation campaign, Lincoln Mitchell, GSAS ’96, political analyst and professor at the School of International and Public Affairs, said that Miller’s involvement in pulling funding from Columbia is proof that “this comes from the absolute worst of the worst in the Trump administration.”

‘Immense relief, but also cautious’

About 80 percent of Columbia’s research grants come from HHS, with only a small number from the Department of Education. More than half of the federal funding that Columbia lost in March 2025 came from the National Institutes of Health, which is part of HHS—$250 million in the form of more than 400 grants that dozens of Columbia researchers depended on for pivotal projects.

Jessie Ford, assistant professor of sociomedical sciences at the Mailman School of Public Health whose $800,000 K01 grant was affected by the funding cuts, told Spectator that the news of the deal triggered “immense relief” but stressed she was also “cautious” about the implications.

[Read more: ‘Everyone feels insecure’: One year since $400 million federal funding cuts, Columbia researchers report lingering uncertainty]

“There was a sense of relief that that money was going to be restored,” Christopher Baldassano, associate professor of psychology, said. “I was also very worried about what this meant about the relationship between Columbia and the federal government going forward in terms of whether this was money that was fair for Columbia to pay.”

As funding uncertainty set in the early months of Columbia’s standoff with the federal government, Columbia announced two research stabilization funds in May 2025 to inject life into stalled projects.

Ford and Baldassano both applied to the fund and received roughly $150,000 and $100,000, respectively.

Baldassano said the fund “made an enormous difference” for his lab, with the money allowing for staff to be paid, the preliminary collection of data to continue, and for at least one of the Ph.D. students he oversaw to finish his work to be able to defend his thesis. Ford said the funds were an example of feeling supported by Columbia.

While lauding these internal efforts to support researchers, Joseph Howley, associate professor of classics, stressed how monumental it would have been for Columbia to mount a legal challenge to the funding cuts.

“I’m glad that the University moved internally to try to support researchers as much as they could,” Howley said, “but in failing to mount any kind of public, political, or legal challenge to these funding cuts or defense of these researchers and their work, the University missed a crucial opportunity to defend an institution and a way of doing things in this country that has made the United States a powerhouse in academic research.”

‘We weren’t a house united’

For Howley and Anya Schiffrin, Journalism ’00, a senior lecturer at SIPA, fault lines within Columbia created an environment conducive to outside oversight.

“I always thought of us as a community that would stand up for academic freedom and stand up for governance, and now I just feel I was so naive,” Schiffrin said. She later noted, “We weren’t a house united,” because University administrators decided to follow advice that was “inconsistent with the principles of standing up that I would have thought we would follow.”

Howley said, “It’s a real mistake to continue to think about this in terms of Columbia’s response to federal scrutiny and not think about how Columbia invited federal scrutiny.”

One example of that internal pressure predates the federal demands themselves. A February 2025 letter to then-interim University President Katrina Armstrong, signed by nearly 200 faculty members, called on the University to implement 10 recommendations aimed at protecting Jewish students, including a mask ban. Columbia would go on to implement a partial mask ban in its March 21, 2025, response to federal demands. A week before the settlement, it would adopt the International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance’s definition of antisemitism.

A spokesperson for the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, which oversees a $21 million claimant fund included in the settlement for Columbia affiliates who experienced antisemitism on campus, labeled the response to the claims process as “robust” in a July 15 statement to Spectator, adding that eligible claimants will begin receiving notifications later this year. The agency said it “remains committed to addressing antisemitism and religious discrimination in all workplaces.”

[Read more: Columbia will pay Jewish employees who experienced antisemitism $21 million as part of its government settlement. Here’s how it works, according to the EEOC’s acting chair.]

Similarly, since September 2024, Columbia had been planning on expanding the power of its Public Safety officers. Its March 21, 2025, partial compliance with demands from the Trump administration announced the hiring of at least 36 special patrol officers with arrest powers for the next three years.

“There’s a kind of general sense that many of the demands made by the Trump administration were fed to them from within Columbia,” Fortna said.

The University did not directly address these criticisms in its statement to Spectator.

That internal-external overlap, some faculty argue, reflects a deeper leadership problem. Jean Howard, professor emerita of the humanities, said ineffective leadership and constant turnover created openings for federal scrutiny.

“I’m not going to blame people inside the University for what Trump did to us,” Howard said, “but I am disappointed that in responding to Trump, our administration and our board of trustees seems to have mimetically imitated many of his autocratic ways of dealing with problems, and the reduction of faculty influence on these major decisions is one of the great catastrophes of these two years.”

Another instance in which faculty members argued they felt sidelined was in the creation of an undergraduate major in global public policy at SIPA.

In February, the University’s regional review committee tasked with overseeing the Middle East, South Asian, and African studies department—another key component of the March 21, 2025, list of commitments—issued its first set of recommendations. It called for an expansion of scholarship focused on scholarship on Israel, the Persian Gulf, and Africa, including the creation of an undergraduate major at SIPA focused on social science and policy analysis of the Middle East.

The committee on instruction provisionally approved the global public policy major in December 2025 ahead of its roll out in the 2026-27 academic year.

“You’d have to be born yesterday not to recognize that a key purpose of the SIPA major is to provide curricular offerings to undergraduates that are more sympathetic to Israel,” Michael Thaddeus, professor of mathematics and former vice president of Columbia-AAUP, said.

The University did not directly address this criticism in its statement to Spectator. Additionally, the University did not address Spectator’s request for comment from Miguel Uriquola, senior vice provost for academic initiatives, who is tasked with overseeing the department, on how he assess Columbia’s compliance with the deal.

An FAQ on SIPA’s website states that the school created the major because it “positions Columbia alongside its peers in meeting growing student interest in public policy and global affairs.”

Fortna also touched on the deal calling for the appointment of new faculty members with joint positions in both the Institute for Israel and Jewish Studies and the economics and political science departments or SIPA.

She recognized the “very good reasons” for hiring individuals who specialize in Israeli politics, calling the field “super interesting” and noting Columbia currently lacks anyone who works on it, but she raised concerns about optics.

“I’m not opposed to us hiring somebody,” she acknowledged, “but the fact that it was named in the agreement actually makes it harder for us to do that in an open way and in a way that maintains academic freedom.”

The University did not directly address this criticism in its statement to Spectator.

Given these underlying internal fractures, Columbia, in signing the deal, externally cemented its decision to embark on a different path than Harvard by choosing to settle instead of suing the federal government.

Harvard’s $56.9 billion endowment—more than triple Columbia’s $15.9 billion—gave it a stronger financial position. Columbia announced in fall 2025 that it had taken the unprecedented step of tapping into unrestricted endowment funds to mitigate the losses that stemmed from federal funding cuts.

According to an FAQ sheet about the deal released by the University, although it was considered, Columbia chose not to file a lawsuit against the government because “while we might have gained short-term victories in court, it likely would have come at a steep price, incurring deeper, long-term damage.” It argued that the damages included losing eligibility for future funding, losing accreditation, and the potential revocation of international students’ visas, and would not have solved pending investigations.

Martin and Howard believe Columbia should have sued, like Harvard, or joined the AAUP and the American Federation of Teachers’ lawsuit against the federal government over the canceled funds—a case declared moot in May once the settlement restored the money.

“I realize that it’s a very hard thing to do,” Howard said. “It takes resources, and it takes courage, and it takes a kind of leadership we just didn’t have.”

‘President Shipman owns this agreement’

The settlement was one of Shipman’s landmark decisions in her 15-month tenure as Columbia’s acting president—one characterized by clashes with internal and external actors over the direction of the University.

“President Shipman owns this agreement,” Thaddeus said. “It happened on her watch. It’s one of the very consequential things that she did.”

Since the deal’s signing, the University has continued carrying out its terms. In April, Columbia released the first of several semiannual compliance reports. Notably, the University announced it would require a civil discourse attestation from incoming students by this fall and appoint a “student liaison” to oversee the “wellbeing of Jewish students.” Michelle Greenberg-Kobrin, CC ’96, Law ’99, was tapped for the role in June.

The report cited antidiscrimination trainings and mandatory participation in Columbia’s Community Citizenship Initiative as evidence of civil discourse efforts.

[Read more: Columbia mandated RAs to lead a new pluralism initiative. RAs said it made them political pawns.]

Fortna recognized the importance of civil discourse but argued that the need for “infrastructure” to have dialogue is “a little bit troubling.”

“It assumes that we, as a community, were not able to have civil discourse before, and I, at least from what I see in my department, in my classrooms, I don’t think that was true,” Fortna added.

One year since the deal, while direct federal action against Columbia has eased, its peers remain under scrutiny.

Harvard continues fighting against the federal government through multiple lawsuits and investigations. Yale University—one of the few Ivy League institutions left unscathed by the Trump administration’s oversight mechanisms last year—is in talks to settle with the federal government to end the DOJ’s May investigation into the Yale School of Medicine’s admissions practices. And on Wednesday, the Department of Education announced a similar investigation into Dartmouth College’s Geisel School of Medicine.

The investigations come months after a federal judge blocked an effort by the department to access six years of admissions data from over 170 colleges, including Columbia and Barnard, starting from the 2019-20 academic year.

The settlement requires Columbia to maintain “merit-based admissions policies” and notes that the federal government is not barred from conducting additional “compliance reviews, investigations, or litigation into Columbia’s future admissions practices” to ensure it follows the law. Columbia sent over “an initial set of information” pertinent to admissions data in October 2025.

“I worry a lot about admissions because it’s not a science, it’s an art, and raw numbers alone don’t really tell you everything you need to know about what makes for a good class, what makes for fair admissions practices,” Howard said.

[Read more: A history of affirmative action at Columbia and Barnard]

Columbia’s own obligations are far from finished, with several milestones remaining: two more payments of approximately $66.7 million to the federal government and multiple semiannual reports updating officials on provisions that Columbia has not yet fully complied with. The deal will not sunset until July 2028, months before the next presidential election.

The University spokesperson confirmed to Spectator that the next semiannual report will be released in October.

Baldassano hopes that “the remaining two years are as quiet as this first year,” whereas Howard says thinking about how the next two years of Columbia’s compliance could exacerbate existing fractures makes her “very tired.”

“It’s already in terrible shape,” Howard said of Columbia’s current standing. “Its reputation is very damaged by what’s happened, and two more years of this just makes me worry more.”
